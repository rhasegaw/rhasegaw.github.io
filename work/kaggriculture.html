Case study / Kaggle / 2026

A farm. An agent.
A shared economy.

A farming strategy has to balance what it can produce, what it can afford, and what the market will still pay for. Other agents change all three.

01 / The problem

Production was only
half the problem.

Kaggriculture was a two-player farming simulation spanning 30 simulated days and 720 turns. Agents made decisions about crops, animals, fertilizer, land, and sales, with the objective of maximizing final bank coins.

The interesting part was the shared market. Product value depended on inventory, so an attractive production strategy could become less attractive as both players supplied the same goods. A plan had to work against another decision-maker, not just against a fixed environment.

The central trade-off

More output did not automatically mean more money. A useful decision had to account for sale prices, feed and fertilizer needs, travel, storage pressure, and the time left in the game.

02 / The approach

Observe. Plan.
Act. Reconsider.

I developed a closed-loop planner: it read the current observation, maintained player state, built a daily plan, and turned that plan into jobs for the available workers. The next observation could change the priorities.

  1. Read the worldParse the observed farm, player state, and market rather than assume yesterday’s conditions still hold.
  2. Set the daily directionChoose production and spending priorities within limits on crops, animals, reserves, and the remaining horizon.
  3. Generate actionable jobsCreate crop, animal-care, and market tasks from that plan. Include the supporting work needed to keep production running.
  4. Assign by urgency and economic valueWeigh priorities alongside walking cost and whether a worker is already on the relevant tile. Execute, observe the result, and update.

This made the agent a state-dependent decision system rather than a fixed sequence of moves. The architecture separated the longer-term production direction from the immediate question of what each worker should do next.

03 / Economic decisions

Protect the inputs.
Price the outputs.

Decision Planner treatment
What to sell Use inventory-dependent prices, product-specific selling floors, and a priority order for premium products. Overflow selling handled storage pressure as a separate concern.
What to keep Reserve feed and fertilizer so an immediate sale did not leave the production chain without essential inputs.
How far to expand Bound crop production and animal purchases. The retained strategy explored sheep and yarn production alongside feed logistics, with planting cutoffs as the game progressed.
Where to work next Include travel cost and bonuses for work on the current tile, while keeping the economic priority of the job in view.

A particularly useful failure mode was market saturation. Expanding livestock production could look strong against fixed opponents, then lose its advantage when rival strategies expanded supply too. The apparent gain belonged to a particular opponent mix, not to production volume by itself.

04 / Testing & rejected ideas

The smaller metric
wasn’t the objective.

I used local games to compare candidate strategies and inspect why they earned or lost money. The tests had to cover varied game states and opponents; a single successful trajectory was not a transferable policy.

Three ideas that did not earn their place

  • Replaying market orders. A sequence of orders was tied to the state in which it was created. Reusing it in different states caused large losses.
  • Replaying action histories. An action-history or tree-replay approach did not transfer reliably to other games. It could reproduce a path without solving the decision problem that produced it.
  • Optimizing routes in isolation. Reducing walking improved a local efficiency measure, but did not improve held-out game outcomes. That version was not submitted.

How I read the results

Travel reduction, production volume, and replay performance were diagnostic signals. They were not substitutes for the outcome of a complete game against competing strategies.

The official final result was 76th of 10,148 teams and a silver medal. The planner mechanisms above describe project development; the final ranking does not establish the individual contribution of any one rule.

05 / What I took away

A strategy changes
its own environment.

The question was not simply “What can I produce?” It was “What will still be worth producing when the other player acts?”

This competition connected programming to economics in a concrete way. Inventory, production, labor, and opponent behavior formed one system. A rule that helped one part could hurt the final objective.

It also changed how I interpret backtests. I want to know which conditions made a strategy look good, whether those conditions survive different opponents, and whether a measured improvement reaches the actual outcome.

Sharing what I learned

I wrote a beginner guide to Kaggriculture scoring and submission strategy. It received 95 community votes as of 2026-11-09. Its title refers to an earlier public-leaderboard position; my final result is the silver-medal finish reported above.
