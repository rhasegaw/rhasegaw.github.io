01 / Research question

What makes a useful
agent intervention?

A coding agent has to inspect a repository, edit files, test its changes, and decide when to submit a patch. Each action consumes a limited budget. Improving a local measurement only matters if the complete run produces a correct repair.

I investigated two interventions: reallocating the agent’s inference time and editing its submission decisions with LoRA. The work combines runtime analysis, a memory-efficient editing implementation, controlled evaluation tasks, and paired comparisons of complete trajectories.

Central finding

An edit can preserve most ordinary-token outputs while changing when an agent submits and whether it stops afterward. Those properties need separate measurements, followed by repair tests.

02 / Completed study

Allocating a fixed
inference budget.

I fitted a time model using agent steps and generated tokens, then checked its transfer by holding out entire repositories. Across 78 runs, the combined model had a mean absolute timing error of 13.5 seconds, compared with 28.2 seconds for tokens alone. These estimates use the steps and tokens observed in a run; they do not predict an unknown future trajectory.

I then varied thinking budgets, tool-call limits, and model-turn limits. One configuration removed timeouts on an eight-issue local comparison while retaining three repairs, but its public competition score fell from 0.15 to 0.08. Timing estimates also transferred poorly when thinking mode changed.

The result identifies a practical evaluation requirement: measure both resource use and repair quality, and recalibrate the cost model when the execution mode changes.

03 / Method development

Editing a 31B model
with limited GPU memory.

I captured intermediate representations one decoder layer at a time, keeping hidden states on CPU. This allowed the editing procedure to use a single RTX 5060 with 8 GB of GPU memory, without placing the full model on that GPU.

With one LoRA factor fixed, I derived and implemented a closed-form refit of the other. A regularized quadratic objective balances retaining the edit at 60 decision positions against suppressing its effect at 18,927 ordinary positions.

  1. Formulate the optimization Differentiate the objective with respect to the matrix factor and solve its stationary condition. Check the positive-definite Hessian conditions that establish a unique global minimum.
  2. Reuse the expensive computation Cache an eigendecomposition and use the Woodbury identity to exploit the small number of decision rows during refitting.
  3. Measure effects beyond the fitting objective Compare intended submission decisions, false submissions, ordinary-token agreement, termination, and actual repairs.

The refit reached 98.97% agreement with the base model on the ordinary-token evaluation, yet false submissions increased from 1 of 38 to 14 of 38 serving-probe cases. Preserving token choices did not preserve the agent’s use of actions.

From an objective to an implementation

Let A be the original input factor and X its replacement. The positive-semidefinite matrices Cd and Cg summarize inputs at decision and ordinary positions. For λ ≥ 0 and ε > 0, I minimized:

ℒ(X) = tr((X − A)Cd(X − A)T)
+ λ tr(XCgXT) + ε ‖X‖F2.

The first term retains the existing edit at decision positions; the second penalizes adapter activity at ordinary positions. The ridge term makes the stationary system invertible even if the data moments are singular. Writing M = Cd + λCg + εI:

∇ℒ(X) = 2(XM − ACd)
X* = ACdM−1.

A zero gradient alone is insufficient. Each row has Hessian 2M, and vTMv ≥ ε‖v‖2 for every nonzero v, establishing a unique global minimum. The exact perturbation identity ℒ(X* + H) − ℒ(X*) = tr(HMHT) describes curvature of this fitting loss. It does not establish preservation of the model’s actions.

I implemented the refit as a Python Refitter object that owns a cached eigensystem. With at most 60 decision rows, the Woodbury identity reduces the parameter-dependent solve to at most 60 × 60, even when the input dimension is 21,504. Dense moments and eigenvectors still require quadratic storage; the final basis transformation also remains quadratic in input dimension.

Inspect the refit calculation

This excerpt from Refitter.__call__ uses the eigenvalues and decision projection cached during initialization. Here self.w contains the eigenvalues of Cg, and the positive ridge parameter is ε = c * self.w.mean().

g = lam * self.w + c * float(self.w.mean())
UtGinv_V = self.UtV / g
S = UtGinv_V @ self.UtV.T
AU = A.astype(np.float64) @ self.U
core = AU @ np.linalg.solve(np.eye(len(S)) + S, UtGinv_V)
return (core @ self.V.T.astype(np.float64)).astype(np.float32)

The implementation solves the reduced system instead of explicitly forming the full inverse shown in the derivation.

Checking the derivation

Thirteen existing synthetic checks cover the finite-difference gradient, stationary solution, exact Taylor identity, penalty sensitivity, Woodbury/direct-solve agreement, and related identities. The measured Woodbury/direct-solve discrepancy was 3.51 × 10−16; a separate mixed-precision implementation check had discrepancy 6.21 × 10−8, below its 2 × 10−6 tolerance. These checks validate the algebra and its numerical implementation; the repair experiments test the resulting agent.

Further analysis: sensitivity and convergence

Holding the data moments and ε fixed, differentiating the stationary equation gives dX*/dλ = −X*CgM−1. This measures local sensitivity of the refitted factor to the preservation penalty.

The supplementary analysis also represents the inverse as an improper integral and a geometric series:

M−1 = ∫0∞ exp(−tM) dt
M−1 = α ∑k=0∞ (I − αM)k.

The series requires 0 < α < 2/μmax, where μmax is the largest eigenvalue of M. With q = maxj|1 − αμj| < 1, truncation after term K has spectral-norm error at most αqK+1/(1 − q). These are analyses of the solution; the reported refits used the eigensystem and reduced solve above.

04 / Completed prospective replication

Test the behavior.
Then grade the repair.

I tested the frozen edit on 36 fresh issues from SymPy, Sphinx, xarray, pylint, and pytest. These repositories were excluded from edit development. Task selection, execution order, code and weight hashes, and the primary hypothesis were recorded before the first inference run.

Four conditions separate the model edit from a submission guard: original or refitted weights, each with the guard off or on. The 144 main executions were supplemented by eight repeat runs on four preselected issues. Each run used a 300-second limit, 80 counted tool calls, and 80 model turns.

Without the guard, the pre-specified submission signature—an empty first submission or at least three generated submission calls—occurred in 13 of 36 refit runs and none of the paired base runs. A two-sided exact McNemar test on the discordant pairs gave p = 0.000244. The base model nevertheless reached its time limit in 33 of 36 runs; the absence of that signature does not imply good termination.

Pairing matters: each base run and refit run faced the same issue. Among the 13 discordant pairs, every event occurred in the refit condition. Under the paired null, the two-sided probability is 2 × (1/2)13 = 0.000244. This tests the submission signature; it does not turn that signature into a measure of repair quality.

Repair grading used the official Evaluator on Kaggle, with passing gold-patch and failing empty-patch controls. The study reproduced the submission anomaly in a different runtime and scaffold, but did not establish improved repair performance. Its scope is the same 31B model and frozen edit, not a demonstration across model families.

Reproducibility

The submission includes task definitions, predictions, traces, grading logs, and executable analyses. A hosted CPU notebook completed 148 analysis checks. Reproducing those analyses is separate from rerunning 31B inference.

05 / Supplementary algorithm study

Make the matching rule
exact and testable.

A separate audit asks whether a generated patch contains a long token span shared with a reference patch but absent, as a whole, from each supplied context text. The earlier matching-block heuristic could miss valid spans after selecting a longer, excluded match. I implemented an exact matcher using the established suffix-automaton data structure.

The rule and why it works

At each ending position i in the generated tokens, let Lg[i] be the longest suffix found in the reference, and Lb[i] the longest suffix found in any excluded text. Because occurrences include every shorter suffix, the longest valid span at that position is Lg[i] exactly when Lg[i] > Lb[i]. Otherwise, none is valid there. Taking the maximum over positions gives the exact answer.

An existing validation case

Generated: n1 n2 n3 b1 b2 b3 b4
Reference: b1 b2 b3 b4 n1 n2 n3
Excluded:  b1 b2 b3 b4

Exact eligible span: n1 n2 n3
Length: 3; earlier heuristic: 0

The four-token match is excluded, but the three-token match remains eligible. The exact matcher examines both rather than relying on the first alignment.

Data structures and object boundaries

Each automaton stores transitions in dictionaries and state lengths and suffix links in lists. The invariant len(link(v)) < l ≤ len(v) ties the current nonzero match length to its state. Separate classes encapsulate the automaton, excluded-text corpus, comparison rule, and cache; the auditor composes these objects through matching-profile interfaces.

For a fixed number of excluded texts, the core matcher has expected linear time and space in input size under average-case hash-table operations. The complete audit additionally runs legacy and dynamic-programming cross-checks, so its total runtime is not claimed to be linear.

Independent checks and recorded results

Synthetic validation compares the automaton with exhaustive substring enumeration and a separate dynamic-programming recurrence. The completed audit of 81 earlier patches also cross-checks each result against that recurrence. Ten reference-match lengths and three control-span lengths increased. The 40-token flag count stayed at 3/81 for reference patches and zero for controls.

A flag is a signal for investigation, not proof of copying. This later audit supplements the earlier study; it does not redefine the endpoints of the 36-issue replication.

06 / Updated November 11, 2026

Research status
and next questions.

The report was submitted to the Gemma 4 Developer Agent Paper competition on November 11, 2026. The 152-run replication and repair grading are complete. Public access and a DOI are pending.

The continuing research direction is to understand which measurements reveal an agent’s behavior before deployment. Follow-up questions include:

  • Whether explicitly training against false submissions and testing post-submission states can improve action specificity without sacrificing correct repairs.
  • Whether the same failure pattern appears in other models and execution environments.
  • How to recalibrate resource budgets across reasoning modes while preserving repair quality.

The submitted materials include the derivation, editing code, task definitions, traces, grading logs, and analysis notebook. The public report and supporting resources will be linked here when available. The follow-up questions above remain open.
